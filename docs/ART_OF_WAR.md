# The Art of War: source rules and principles

Source: Sun Tzu, *The Art of War*, translated by Lionel Giles (1910), public domain, [Project Gutenberg #132](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/132). Commentary removed; references are `chapter.verse`.

- **Part 1** is the key passages, quoted word for word.
- **Part 2** is the main principles distilled from them, numbered `P1`, `P2`... so the design docs and code comments can cite them.

## Part 1: The rules, as written

### I. Laying Plans

- **I.3–4** The art of war, then, is governed by five constant factors... These are: (1) The Moral Law; (2) Heaven; (3) Earth; (4) The Commander; (5) Method and discipline.
- **I.7** *Heaven* signifies night and day, cold and heat, times and seasons.
- **I.8** *Earth* comprises distances, great and small; danger and security; open ground and narrow passes; the chances of life and death.
- **I.10** By *Method and discipline* are to be understood the marshalling of the army in its proper subdivisions, the gradations of rank among the officers, the maintenance of roads by which supplies may reach the army, and the control of military expenditure.
- **I.13** (1) Which of the two sovereigns is imbued with the Moral law? (2) Which of the two generals has most ability? (3) With whom lie the advantages derived from Heaven and Earth? (4) On which side is discipline most rigorously enforced? (5) Which army is the stronger? (6) On which side are officers and men more highly trained? (7) In which army is there the greater constancy both in reward and punishment?
- **I.14** By means of these seven considerations I can forecast victory or defeat.
- **I.17** According as circumstances are favourable, one should modify one's plans.
- **I.18** All warfare is based on deception.
- **I.19** Hence, when able to attack, we must seem unable; when using our forces, we must seem inactive; when we are near, we must make the enemy believe we are far away; when far away, we must make him believe we are near.
- **I.20** Hold out baits to entice the enemy. Feign disorder, and crush him.
- **I.21** If he is secure at all points, be prepared for him. If he is in superior strength, evade him.
- **I.23** If he is taking his ease, give him no rest. If his forces are united, separate them.
- **I.24** Attack him where he is unprepared, appear where you are not expected.
- **I.26** Now the general who wins a battle makes many calculations in his temple ere the battle is fought. The general who loses a battle makes but few calculations beforehand. Thus do many calculations lead to victory, and few calculations to defeat: how much more no calculation at all!

### II. Waging War

- **II.2** When you engage in actual fighting, if victory is long in coming, the men's weapons will grow dull and their ardour will be damped. If you lay siege to a town, you will exhaust your strength.
- **II.5** Thus, though we have heard of stupid haste in war, cleverness has never been seen associated with long delays.
- **II.6** There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare.
- **II.9** Bring war material with you from home, but forage on the enemy.
- **II.15** Hence a wise general makes a point of foraging on the enemy. One cartload of the enemy's provisions is equivalent to twenty of one's own.
- **II.18** This is called, using the conquered foe to augment one's own strength.
- **II.19** In war, then, let your great object be victory, not lengthy campaigns.

### III. Attack by Stratagem

- **III.2** Hence to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting.
- **III.3** Thus the highest form of generalship is to baulk the enemy's plans; the next best is to prevent the junction of the enemy's forces; the next in order is to attack the enemy's army in the field; and the worst policy of all is to besiege walled cities.
- **III.4** The rule is, not to besiege walled cities if it can possibly be avoided.
- **III.8** It is the rule in war, if our forces are ten to the enemy's one, to surround him; if five to one, to attack him; if twice as numerous, to divide our army into two.
- **III.9** If equally matched, we can offer battle; if slightly inferior in numbers, we can avoid the enemy; if quite unequal in every way, we can flee from him.
- **III.10** Hence, though an obstinate fight may be made by a small force, in the end it must be captured by the larger force.
- **III.17** Thus we may know that there are five essentials for victory: (1) He will win who knows when to fight and when not to fight. (2) He will win who knows how to handle both superior and inferior forces. (3) He will win whose army is animated by the same spirit throughout all its ranks. (4) He will win who, prepared himself, waits to take the enemy unprepared. (5) He will win who has military capacity and is not interfered with by the sovereign.
- **III.18** If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat. If you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will succumb in every battle.

### IV. Tactical Dispositions

- **IV.1** The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity of defeating the enemy.
- **IV.2** To secure ourselves against defeat lies in our own hands, but the opportunity of defeating the enemy is provided by the enemy himself.
- **IV.11** What the ancients called a clever fighter is one who not only wins, but excels in winning with ease.
- **IV.13** He wins his battles by making no mistakes. Making no mistakes is what establishes the certainty of victory, for it means conquering an enemy that is already defeated.
- **IV.14** Hence the skilful fighter puts himself into a position which makes defeat impossible, and does not miss the moment for defeating the enemy.
- **IV.15** Thus it is that in war the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won, whereas he who is destined to defeat first fights and afterwards looks for victory.
- **IV.17** In respect of military method, we have, firstly, Measurement; secondly, Estimation of quantity; thirdly, Calculation; fourthly, Balancing of chances; fifthly, Victory.

### V. Energy

- **V.4** That the impact of your army may be like a grindstone dashed against an egg—this is effected by the science of weak points and strong.
- **V.5** In all fighting, the direct method may be used for joining battle, but indirect methods will be needed in order to secure victory.
- **V.10** In battle, there are not more than two methods of attack—the direct and the indirect; yet these two in combination give rise to an endless series of manœuvers.
- **V.13** The quality of decision is like the well-timed swoop of a falcon which enables it to strike and destroy its victim.
- **V.14** Therefore the good fighter will be terrible in his onset, and prompt in his decision.
- **V.15** Energy may be likened to the bending of a crossbow; decision, to the releasing of the trigger.
- **V.19** He sacrifices something, that the enemy may snatch at it.
- **V.20** By holding out baits, he keeps him on the march; then with a body of picked men he lies in wait for him.
- **V.21** The clever combatant looks to the effect of combined energy, and does not require too much from individuals.

### VI. Weak Points and Strong

- **VI.1** Whoever is first in the field and awaits the coming of the enemy, will be fresh for the fight; whoever is second in the field and has to hasten to battle, will arrive exhausted.
- **VI.2** Therefore the clever combatant imposes his will on the enemy, but does not allow the enemy's will to be imposed on him.
- **VI.5** Appear at points which the enemy must hasten to defend; march swiftly to places where you are not expected.
- **VI.6** An army may march great distances without distress, if it marches through country where the enemy is not.
- **VI.7** You can be sure of succeeding in your attacks if you only attack places which are undefended. You can ensure the safety of your defence if you only hold positions that cannot be attacked.
- **VI.10** You may advance and be absolutely irresistible, if you make for the enemy's weak points; you may retire and be safe from pursuit if your movements are more rapid than those of the enemy.
- **VI.11** If we wish to fight, the enemy can be forced to an engagement even though he be sheltered behind a high rampart and a deep ditch. All we need do is attack some other place that he will be obliged to relieve.
- **VI.13** By discovering the enemy's dispositions and remaining invisible ourselves, we can keep our forces concentrated, while the enemy's must be divided.
- **VI.17** If he sends reinforcements everywhere, he will everywhere be weak.
- **VI.24** Carefully compare the opposing army with your own, so that you may know where strength is superabundant and where it is deficient.
- **VI.28** Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances.
- **VI.30** So in war, the way is to avoid what is strong and to strike at what is weak.
- **VI.32** Therefore, just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions.

### VII. Manœuvering

- **VII.4** Thus, to take a long and circuitous route, after enticing the enemy out of the way, and though starting after him, to contrive to reach the goal before him, shows knowledge of the artifice of *deviation*.
- **VII.11** We may take it then that an army without its baggage-train is lost; without provisions it is lost; without bases of supply it is lost.
- **VII.13** We are not fit to lead an army on the march unless we are familiar with the face of the country.
- **VII.15** In war, practise dissimulation, and you will succeed. Move only if there is a real advantage to be gained.
- **VII.16** Whether to concentrate or to divide your troops, must be decided by circumstances.
- **VII.17–19** Let your rapidity be that of the wind, your compactness that of the forest. In raiding and plundering be like fire, in immovability like a mountain. Let your plans be dark and impenetrable as night, and when you move, fall like a thunderbolt.
- **VII.29** A clever general, therefore, avoids an army when its spirit is keen, but attacks it when it is sluggish and inclined to return.
- **VII.31** To be near the goal while the enemy is still far from it, to wait at ease while the enemy is toiling and struggling, to be well-fed while the enemy is famished:—this is the art of husbanding one's strength.
- **VII.34–36** Do not pursue an enemy who simulates flight; do not attack soldiers whose temper is keen. Do not swallow a bait offered by the enemy. Do not interfere with an army that is returning home. When you surround an army, leave an outlet free. Do not press a desperate foe too hard.

### VIII. Variation of Tactics

- **VIII.3** There are roads which must not be followed, armies which must be not attacked, towns which must not be besieged, positions which must not be contested.
- **VIII.7** Hence in the wise leader's plans, considerations of advantage and of disadvantage will be blended together.
- **VIII.11** The art of war teaches us to rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him; not on the chance of his not attacking, but rather on the fact that we have made our position unassailable.
- **VIII.12** There are five dangerous faults which may affect a general: (1) Recklessness, which leads to destruction; (2) cowardice, which leads to capture; (3) a hasty temper, which can be provoked by insults; (4) a delicacy of honour which is sensitive to shame; (5) over-solicitude for his men, which exposes him to worry and trouble.

### IX. The Army on the March

- **IX.4** When an invading force crosses a river in its onward march, do not advance to meet it in mid-stream. It will be best to let half the army get across, and then deliver your attack.
- **IX.17** ...woods with thick undergrowth, they must be carefully routed out and searched; for these are places where men in ambush or insidious spies are likely to be lurking.
- **IX.20** If his place of encampment is easy of access, he is tendering a bait.
- **IX.31** If the enemy sees an advantage to be gained and makes no effort to secure it, the soldiers are exhausted.
- **IX.40** If our troops are no more in number than the enemy, that is amply sufficient; it only means that no direct attack can be made. What we can do is simply to concentrate all our available strength, keep a close watch on the enemy, and obtain reinforcements.
- **IX.41** He who exercises no forethought but makes light of his opponents is sure to be captured by them.

### X. Terrain

- **X.1** We may distinguish six kinds of terrain, to wit: (1) Accessible ground; (2) entangling ground; (3) temporising ground; (4) narrow passes; (5) precipitous heights; (6) positions at a great distance from the enemy.
- **X.4–5** Ground which can be abandoned but is hard to re-occupy is called *entangling*. From a position of this sort, if the enemy is unprepared, you may sally forth and defeat him. But if the enemy is prepared for your coming, and you fail to defeat him, then, return being impossible, disaster will ensue.
- **X.8–9** With regard to *narrow passes*, if you can occupy them first, let them be strongly garrisoned and await the advent of the enemy. Should the enemy forestall you in occupying a pass, do not go after him if the pass is fully garrisoned, but only if it is weakly garrisoned.
- **X.19** When a general, unable to estimate the enemy's strength, allows an inferior force to engage a larger one, or hurls a weak detachment against a powerful one... the result must be a *rout*.
- **X.21** The natural formation of the country is the soldier's best ally; but a power of estimating the adversary, of controlling the forces of victory, and of shrewdly calculating difficulties, dangers and distances, constitutes the test of a great general.
- **X.23** If fighting is sure to result in victory, then you must fight, even though the ruler forbid it; if fighting will not result in victory, then you must not fight even at the ruler's bidding.
- **X.27–29** If we know that our own men are in a condition to attack, but are unaware that the enemy is not open to attack, we have gone only halfway towards victory. If we know that the enemy is open to attack, but are unaware that our own men are not in a condition to attack, we have gone only halfway towards victory. If we know that the enemy is open to attack, and also know that our men are in a condition to attack, but are unaware that the nature of the ground makes fighting impracticable, we have still gone only halfway towards victory.
- **X.31** If you know the enemy and know yourself, your victory will not stand in doubt; if you know Heaven and know Earth, you may make your victory complete.

### XI. The Nine Situations

- **XI.1** The art of war recognises nine varieties of ground: (1) Dispersive ground; (2) facile ground; (3) contentious ground; (4) open ground; (5) ground of intersecting highways; (6) serious ground; (7) difficult ground; (8) hemmed-in ground; (9) desperate ground.
- **XI.11–14** On dispersive ground, therefore, fight not. On facile ground, halt not. On contentious ground, attack not. On open ground, do not try to block the enemy's way. On ground of intersecting highways, join hands with your allies. On serious ground, gather in plunder. In difficult ground, keep steadily on the march. On hemmed-in ground, resort to stratagem. On desperate ground, fight.
- **XI.15** Those who were called skilful leaders of old knew how to drive a wedge between the enemy's front and rear; to prevent co-operation between his large and small divisions; to hinder the good troops from rescuing the bad.
- **XI.18** Begin by seizing something which your opponent holds dear; then he will be amenable to your will.
- **XI.19** Rapidity is the essence of war: take advantage of the enemy's unreadiness, make your way by unexpected routes, and attack unguarded spots.
- **XI.29** The skilful tactician may be likened to the *shuai-jan*... Strike at its head, and you will be attacked by its tail; strike at its tail, and you will be attacked by its head; strike at its middle, and you will be attacked by head and tail both.
- **XI.37** By altering his arrangements and changing his plans, he keeps the enemy without definite knowledge.
- **XI.65** If the enemy leaves a door open, you must rush in.
- **XI.68** At first, then, exhibit the coyness of a maiden, until the enemy gives you an opening; afterwards emulate the rapidity of a running hare, and it will be too late for the enemy to oppose you.

### XII. The Attack by Fire

- **XII.1** There are five ways of attacking with fire. The first is to burn soldiers in their camp; the second is to burn stores; the third is to burn baggage-trains; the fourth is to burn arsenals and magazines; the fifth is to hurl dropping fire amongst the enemy.
- **XII.6–8** When fire breaks out inside the enemy's camp, respond at once with an attack from without. If there is an outbreak of fire, but the enemy's soldiers remain quiet, bide your time and do not attack. When the force of the flames has reached its height, follow it up with an attack, if that is practicable; if not, stay where you are.
- **XII.17** Move not unless you see an advantage; use not your troops unless there is something to be gained; fight not unless the position is critical.
- **XII.18** No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique.
- **XII.21–22** But a kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life. Hence the enlightened ruler is heedful, and the good general full of caution.

### XIII. The Use of Spies

- **XIII.4** Thus, what enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is *foreknowledge*.
- **XIII.6** Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.
- **XIII.7** Hence the use of spies, of whom there are five classes: (1) Local spies; (2) inward spies; (3) converted spies; (4) doomed spies; (5) surviving spies.
- **XIII.12** Having *doomed spies*, doing certain things openly for purposes of deception, and allowing our own spies to know of them and report them to the enemy.
- **XIII.27** Spies are a most important element in war, because on them depends an army's ability to move.
