something the text doesn't. Tags: **[pm]** = the postmortem says it, **[pm-code]** = their code does it (their game, not

  `docs/redo/sources/`).
- **Code:** https://github.com/uravt/Battlecode26. `src/Version40` is the version the PDF links to; `Version41`
  differs only in package names in the files read here. Read from a scratch clone, not copied into the repo.

**Their game (BC 2026, "Uneasy Alliances"):**
- Dozens of 1×1 baby rats, plus 3×3 rat kings that act as the base.
- A forward vision cone.
- Diggable dirt walls.
- Cheese mines, and neutral cats.
- 17,500 bytecode a turn.
- The map is unknown at the start; units learn it by seeing it, and symmetry fills in the rest.

## 1. In short

1. **Their pathfinding section is all about moving a unit (§4).** Where units go is in the macro section and in the
   behaviour code. By their own account they didn't work hard on it: "we don't try too hard to be original … reverse
   engineer their macro … copy whatever strategy we gleaned" (§5.1) [pm].
2. **Their routing reacts; it never plans.**
   - Each turn a priority list picks the behaviour.
   - The target is the nearest or a random known mine, or a point on the map edge.
   - The team spreads out through spawn direction and randomness, with no assignment.
   [pm-code]
3. **For us:**
   - One method carries over fully: copy the routing of the teams above us from their replays.
   - Two small ideas are worth a test: a per-camp memory of contests, and a check on how firmly we hold a target.
   - One negative result matters most. GST never had to plan a tour, because their map, mines and enemies were
     unknown. Ours are known at round 1. The routing problem we face is one their game never posed, so this
     postmortem can't hand us the answer (§6).

## 2. Moving a unit: what the code adds to the text

The text (§4.1–4.2) was covered in `docs/redo/02_GST_REREAD.md` and built as R2 (`docs/redo/05_PATHFINDING.md`). What
the code shows on top [pm-code]:

| Piece | What the code does (`Pathfinding/Pathfinding.java`, `Pathfinding/BabyRatBFS.java`) |
| --- | --- |
| Window | A generated, unrolled search over a **7×7** box (49 cells). Step costs 10 straight / 14 diagonal, plus `dirtCost = 20` per dirt tile when digging. Any tile with a sensed robot counts as blocked (allies too). Four copies cover: plain, avoid cats, dig, and both. |
| What it picks | The reachable cell in the window with the smallest **squared straight-line distance** to the goal. It then steps toward that cell. |
| "Progress" | `closestDistanceOnPath` holds the best such distance since the goal was set. If this turn's best is not a new record, the search returns null and **bug takes over**. So progress means "a new record closeness to the goal", which is Om Nom's rule. |
| Reset | All bug state and the record are reset **when the goal changes, or every 100 rounds** (`RESET_THRESHOLD`). |
| Bug | Keeps a stack of up to 8 directions; if the stack fills, it stops for the turn. The first time it turns at a tile, it simulates 5 steps going left and 5 going right over the remembered passability, and keeps the side that ends closer. The next time it turns at the same tile, it takes the other side. Reaching the map edge also flips the side. |
| Looking while walking | When the goal is more than 4 tiles away (squared distance > 16), the bot alternates move and turn and swings its cone right, then left. This is information gathering, built into the walk. |
| Rat king | It has its own 3×3-footprint search. In headquarters mode it uses a plain greedy "towards, else ±45°, else ±90°" step. |

**The one point here that is about routing:** the progress record resets whenever the goal changes. A unit whose
target flips keeps losing its anti-loop guard. That is the same coupling R2 found: trip rounds didn't move because of
target flips, not because of how a body steps (05 §6.1) [measured]. Moving and routing are not independent; churn in
the routing undoes the guard in the moving.

## 3. Routing: where units go (code)

### 3.1 Picking the behaviour: an ordered list, not a score

`BabyRatStateUpdaters/DefectBRStateUpdater.java`: the first rule that matches wins [pm-code].

1. Being carried or thrown.
2. Answer a rat king in distress within squared distance 25, if there are enemies or cats.
3. An enemy seen, or within squared distance 9 → micro.
4. A cat seen, in cooperation mode or after round 1,700 → cat micro.
5. Symmetry found but not yet in the shared array → walk and report it.
6. A new rat-king formation site is set → go and form it, unless there are already 2 kings after round 1,200, or an
   enemy was seen in the last 2 turns.
7. Cheese seen, carrying ≥ 20, or a mine targeted → scavenge or return.
8. Otherwise → explore.

The rat king: flee if in distress, otherwise headquarters [pm-code].

### 3.2 Targets

| Behaviour | Target, and when it changes [pm-code] |
| --- | --- |
| **Explore** (`BRExplore`) | **Set once:** the direction away from the nearest own rat king. **Target:** the point where that ray meets the map edge. **Within ~4 tiles of the edge:** a random new direction from the 5 that don't point ahead. Moves with cat avoidance on. |
| **Pick a mine** (in explore, after round 75 once symmetry is known) | **Each explore turn, re-rolled:** 30 % the nearest known mine within ±45° of the explore direction, 70 % **a uniformly random known mine**. **Held** until within squared distance 4. A threat-weighted choice (`weightedClosestCheeseMine`: weight 2048/(d+16)+2, scaled down by threat) exists but isn't called in v40. |
| **Collect** (`BRScavengeRatPickup`) | **Target:** the richest cheese tile in vision, nearest on ties. |
| **Return** | **When:** carrying ≥ 100 always; or carrying ≥ 20 when the cheese in sight is gone (`RETURN_CHEESE_THRESHOLD`, `ABSOLUTE_RETURN_CHEESE_THRESHOLD`). **Target:** the nearest own rat king. |
| **Taxi** (§5.2) | **Pick-up:** an ally next to it carrying ≥ 100, when the taxi is more than squared distance 35 from the king and carries ≤ 40 itself. **Drop:** within squared distance 25 of the king. They say it was rare and changed little [pm]. |

### 3.3 Spreading the team without assigning it

There is no assignment of units to mines. The spread comes from three places [pm-code]:

1. **Spawn direction** (`RKHeadquarter.getBestDir`). The king spawns each rat in the direction with the highest
   `(ray length to the edge + centre bias + mine bias) / (rats already sent that way + 1)`. The mine bias (after
   round 150) is +10 for each known mine in that direction and +5 for each in the two directions next to it.
   **Dividing by the count is the load balancer:** each extra rat sent one way lowers the value of that way.
2. **Explore away from the king.** A new rat keeps going roughly the way it was spawned.
3. **Random mine choice:** 70 % of picks are uniform over the known mines.

### 3.4 Memory feeding the routing

- **Every known mine carries a threat count** (`seenMines`). Each sighting adds the enemies and cats within squared
  distance 41 of it. The count only ever grows.
- **Symmetry fills in the enemy side.** Each mine seen also adds its mirror image, marked with threat 127 (treated as
  enemy side).
- **The team learns mines through the king.** After a delivery, a rat squeaks the first mine the king doesn't know,
  or one whose threat has risen, and the king writes it to the shared array [pm-code].
- **Where a new king forms** (`Communicator.getBestFormationLocation`):
  - Skip mines with threat > 60, and mines within squared distance 8 of a king.
  - Score each remaining mine as mean squared distance from the existing kings + (K / number of kings) × 128 / (threat + 1).
  - Take the highest.
  - The chief king asks for a new king when a rat costs more than 1.8× the usual threshold.
- **Where the king itself sits:** it walks to the nearest mine, or to the centre if there is none. It flees heard cats
  and enemies.

**Effect:** the base moves to the resource and new bases open at far, safe mines. That shortens every later return trip.
**Moving the drop-off point is a routing lever.**

## 5. What carries over to CAMBC

Our routing today, for comparison [code, `bots/cpp/src`]:

- **Crews.** Fixed once at the end of the opening (`group_slots`): the richest reachable group per crew, value
  `(gems + gold) / (walk + clear rounds + 1)`.
- **Each turn,** every body prices its options. A farm option is worth `value / bodies / (walk + clear + 5 + altar
  detour) − risk` (`farm_price`).
- **It keeps its current goal** under hysteresis (`HYST`, `farm_hyst`), sunk progress (`SUNK_K`) and a minimum hold
  (`HOLD_TURNS`) (`upd_decide`).
- **Banking** goes to the nearest open altar by walk (`bank_altar`).

| A per-mine threat memory that steers targets and bases | Enemy presence at mines, seen piecemeal | Our price has risk (P(lose) × bag) and an enemy-side flag, but no memory of where we were contested. | Random targets | No comms to coordinate | Every body can compute the same deterministic assignment from the known map. | Don't copy. |

## 6. What GST says about the problem itself

GST's routing is a priority list plus "go to the nearest or a random known thing". In their game that is a sound
choice [pm]:

- **Little is known.** The map, the mines and the enemy are discovered as you go, so a plan made at round 1 is
  mostly wrong by round 50.
- **Units are many and cheap.** Dozens of rats spread by randomness over-cover the mines, and losing one costs little.
- **The value is in reacting.** Cats, ratnapping and digging reward reaction more than planning.

CAMBC flips all three:

- **Everything is known at round 1** [sdk]: the full map, every camp group with its monsters and rewards, the altars,
  and both spawns. Respawn starts when a reward is claimed and its delay is known [rules], so the future supply of
  camps is predictable.
- **We have 3–5 champions, and a death costs 60 rounds** [rules]. Every wasted trip shows in the score.
- **The value is in the order:** which camp next, which one after, and where to bank on the way.

So in CAMBC, routing is a **planning problem** that no 2026 postmortem needed to solve. In its research form it is a
**team orienteering problem with time windows**:

- **Prizes:** the camps' gems and gold.
- **Time windows:** respawns.
- **Team:** 2–3 crews.
- **Depot visits:** altars, priced by what the bag risks.
- **An adversary:** who takes the same prizes.

[guess] on the framing; the mechanics in it are [rules].

**Our router today is greedy and one step deep.** The price is a rate for the *next* camp only. It doesn't see that
camp A leaves the crew next to camp B and an altar, while camp C, slightly richer, leaves it stranded. **Hypothesis
[guess]:** a large part of the 2.25 trip rounds per tile [measured, Python R2] is this lack of lookahead, not the
stepping.

**Test it offline before any design:**
- Replay our crews' realised camp order on each map.
- Compare it with the best 2- and 3-step order from the same states, using the engine distances.
- If the gap is small, the losses are elsewhere (contests, waits for partners, banking).

## 7. What to take into the design

1. **Method:** decode the top teams' ladder replays for their routes: the order of camps, time between camps, who
   moves with whom, and when and where they bank. Their routes are the target; GST's §5.1 is the precedent.
2. **Measure next:** our realised order against a 2–3 step lookahead on the same maps (§6). This decides whether
   routing needs planning or only better pricing.
3. **Small leads:**
   - Per-group contest memory (§5).
   - Choosing the bank altar as part of the route.
   - Keeping the Nav loop memory across goal flips.
4. **Not from GST:** the planner itself. Their game didn't need one. If §7.2 shows a gap, the technique has to come
   from elsewhere (a postmortem from a game with a known map and few units, or the routing literature).
